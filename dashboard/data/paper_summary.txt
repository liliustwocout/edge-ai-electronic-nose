Abstract
Electronic nose systems are a transformative technology, enabling low-cost, real-time gas analysis
for critical applications in environmental monitoring, industrial safety, and medical diagnostics.
However, their practical performance is fundamentally limited by the computational challenge of
analyzing time-series data from their sensor arrays. Conventional machine learning (ML) pipelines
rely on lossy feature engineering and employ separate, inefficient models for gas classification and
concentration regression, a methodology that fundamentally limits predictive accuracy. This paper
presents a unified multitask deep learning model that solves these problems through three key
innovations. First, the model learns features directly from the complete, raw sensor time-series
using a convolutional neural network, avoiding information loss from pre-processing. Second, a
novel attention mechanism links the classification and regression tasks, allowing the model to use
the identified gas type to improve its concentration estimate. Third, a custom loss function was
developed to ensure high accuracy across a wide range of gas concentrations, a critical challenge
in chemical sensing. Using 5-fold cross-validation, the model achieved 100% accuracy in gas clas-
sification and a mean absolute percentage error (MAPE) of 6.61% for concentration regression,
results that significantly outperform classical ML benchmarks. W e also demonstrated the model’s
efficiency by deploying it on 10 different STM32 microcontrollers, confirming its suitability for
real-time embedded applications. This work provides a robust framework for developing high-
performance, AI-based chemical sensors and offers a foundational methodology for future ana-
lysis of complex gas mixtures. The source code and data are provided in this GitHub repo: https://
github.com/quenx-rd/gas_identifier_public.
1.Introduction
Accurate gas detection and quantification are essential in a variety of domains, including environmental
safety, industrial monitoring, and healthcare. T oxic gases such as carbon monoxide (CO), nitrogen diox-
ide (NO 2), and volatile organic compounds pose significant risks to human health and ecosystems [ 1–5].
The detection of specific gases in exhaled breath has enabled early diagnosis of respiratory diseases [ 2–
5]. Conventional methods for gas analysis, such as gas chromatography and mass spectrometry, offer
high accuracy but are often bulky, expensive, and unsuitable for real-time or field deployment [ 6–8].
T o address these limitations, researchers have developed electronic nose (e-nose) systems that emulate
human olfactory processing by integrating sensor arrays with AI-based pattern recognition algorithms
[9–12]. These systems typically rely on a set of gas sensors with partially overlapping sensitivities, allow-
ing them to detect and classify a wide range of gas species.
Metal–oxide–semiconductor (MOS) sensors are widely used in e-nose platforms due to their low
cost, compact form factor, high sensitivity, and ease of integration [ 13–15]. Commercial MOS sensors

such as the MiCS-4514 and MiCS-6814 from SGX Sensortech are popular choices for both academic
and industrial gas sensing applications [ 16]. MOS sensors operate by modulating their electrical resist-
ance in response to gas exposure. Their performance can be enhanced by applying temperature modu-
lation, which varies the sensor’s operating temperature to generate dynamic, time-dependent responses
commonly referred to as thermal fingerprints [ 17–19]. These fingerprints contain rich information
about gas-sensor interactions and can improve the selectivity of a sensor, especially when combined with
machine learning (ML) techniques [ 20–22].
A wide range of traditional ML algorithms has been applied to analyze sensor data and distinguish
between gas types. Common techniques include principal component analysis (PCA) [ 19–21], linear
discriminant analysis (LDA) [ 19, 22–24], support vector machines [ 25, 26], k-nearest neighbors (kNN)
[27], multilayer perceptron (MLP) [ 28], quadratic discriminant analysis (QDA) [ 29], Gaussian Naive
Bayes (GNB) [ 30], random forest (RF) [ 31], decision tree (DT) [ 32], and gradient boosting (GB) [ 25].
These approaches have shown strong potential in classifying gases based on static or dynamic sensor
response patterns. A recent study [ 33] constructed an e-nose with eight MOS sensing elements (from
four MiCS- 4514 chips) and evaluated multiple ML models for classification and regression. Those
approaches are marked by a critical deficiency: a reliance on reductive feature engineering. In this pro-
cess, the raw time-series data is condensed into a small set of handcrafted, static features—such as peak
response, rise time, or integral values derived from dimensionality reduction techniques like PCA. This
is fundamentally a lossy compression, discarding subtle yet discriminative information contained in
the shape and evolution of the sensor response and making the model’s performance highly depend-
ent on the selection of these features. In addition, those pre-processing methods are time consuming
(i.e. PCA) and might not be possible to deploy on low-resource microcontrollers (MCUs) in the e-nose
system. Beyond the inherent limitations of this feature engineering process, the modeling strategy itself
presents a second fundamental deficiency: the disjointed treatment of gas identification and quantific-
ation. The common practice is to train separate models for the classification task (identifying the gas)
and the regression task (estimating its concentration). This separation is not only inefficient, leading to
increased training and deployment overhead, but it is also informationally suboptimal. The regression
model operates in isolation, unaware of the classification output, despite the fact that the physical rela-
tionship between sensor response and concentration is inherently gas-specific.
Recent advancements (2023–2025) have shifted toward deep learning to overcome these deficiencies.
1D-convolutional neural network (CNN) architectures have achieved 100% accuracy in coffee variety
classification [ 34], while lightweight variants like LwCNN enable on-site perfume identification on edge
modules [ 35]. T o capture temporal dependencies, hybrid CNN-BiLSTM models have been deployed for
gas leakage detection [ 36], and federated learning frameworks have been introduced to preserve data
privacy [ 37]. Furthermore, the integration of attention mechanisms, such as in TFA-CNN, allows for
enhanced feature mining in both time and frequency domains for simultaneous gas recognition and con-
centration prediction [ 38]. Lightweight T ransformer-based models like ConvT ran have also demonstrated
state-of-the-art accuracy in capturing global dependencies on embedded e-nose platforms. TinyML has
further enabled real-time diagnostics directly on MCUs for applications like diabetes detection via breath
analysis [ 39].
Despite these advancements, several critical research gaps persist in the existing literature (see
detailed comparison table in table 1). First, there is a scarcity of unified multitask architectures cap-
able of performing both gas classification and concentration regression simultaneously, as these tasks
are often handled by discrete, independent systems. Second, there is a near absence of works integrating
multitask deep learning (MT-DL) with actual MCU deployment for real-time, low-power edge analytics,
which is essential for decentralizing data processing. Finally, the use of rigorous validation procedures,
such as leave-one-concentration-out, to analyze model generalization on completely unseen and non-
overlapping data remains rare in current research.
T o address these challenges, this paper introduces a unified, end-to-end MT-DL framework that dir-
ectly learns from raw thermal fingerprints. Unlike prior work, we eliminate handcrafted features and dis-
jointed pipelines, replacing them with a single deep neural network capable of simultaneous gas classific-
ation and quantification. Our architecture leverages 1D convolutional (Conv1D) networks for automatic
feature extraction and employs a dual-branch multitask design. A novel attention mechanism allows the
classification output to dynamically condition the regression task, ensuring synergistic learning across
both objectives. Furthermore, we propose a concentration-adaptive loss function that maintains high
prediction fidelity across wide dynamic ranges, with particular robustness in low-concentration regimes.
Finally, we validate the framework’s embedded feasibility by deploying it on 10 STM32 MCUs, demon-
strating its practicality for real-world, resource-constrained edge AI applications. The contributions that
realize this methodology are fourfold:

2.Datapreparation
2.1.Experimentalsetup
Figures 1 and 2 show the block diagram a photograph of the actual experimental setup of the gases
measurement system, respectively. The system integrates a sealed sensing chamber within a computer-
controlled continuous flow system, enabling precise delivery of target gases while maintaining a constant
total flow rate [ 33]. The system consists of pressurized gas cylinders connected to mass flow control-
lers (MFCs) and a mass flow control module, which regulates the precise mixing and delivery of target
gases. The conditioned flow is directed into the sealed gas chamber containing the MOS sensor array.
Data acquisition is managed by a dedicated data acquisition board, while a computer equipped with a
LabVIEW software controls the flow sequence, records sensor responses, and synchronizes the measure-
ment cycles. T ogether, these components enable stable, repeatable, and automated operation of the gas
sensing experiments.
2.2.Sensorarraycharacterizationandinter-sensorcorrelation
Gas delivery is regulated by multiple MFCs with maximum capacities of 500, 100, and 10 ml min −1
(±1% accuracy, Kojima Instruments Inc., Kyoto, Japan). Each MFC is connected to a pressurized cyl-
inder containing either clean dry air or a target gas diluted in air. Clean background air is produced
using a compressor, filtration system, and dry air generator, conditioned to 10% relative humidity at
25 ± 1 ◦C. The sensor array consists of four MiCS-4514 chips (SGX Sensortech), providing eight inde-
pendent MOS sensing elements (denoted S1–S8). Each element outputs an electrical resistance that var-
ies with gas exposure. The baseline resistance in clean air is denoted Ra, and the resistance under target
gas exposure is denoted Rg. T o reduce the effect of sensor variability and baseline drift, responses are
normalized as Rg/Ra. Unless otherwise specified, all figures report normalized values.
T arget gases—CO, H2, NH 3, C 2H5OH, CH 3COCH3, H 2S, SO 2, and NO 2 —were individually
introduced and diluted with carrier air to achieve six concentration levels. A fixed total flow rate of
400 ml min −1 was maintained throughout all experiments. A total of eight experiments were conduc-
ted, one for each gas, where each gas was measured continuously across its six concentration levels, as
shown in figure 3. Each target gas was measured once per concentration level, providing a single raw
sample prior to data augmentation. Each concentration measurement cycle consisted of a 90 s exposure
phase followed by a 180 s recovery phase in clean air with sample rating 1 Hz. The transient response is
characterized by a rise behavior during the initial part of the 90 s exposure as the gas concentration in
the chamber stabilizes, while the decay behavior begins immediately after 90 s when clean air is intro-
duced for recovery. Prior to experiments, the sensor array was preheated and stabilized in clean air to
ensure repeatability.
T o characterize the relationships within the sensor array, we computed the Pearson correlation mat-
rix for the complete dataset (figure 4). The analysis reveals a highly structured relationship, identifying
distinct clusters of correlated sensors rather than a set of independent channels. For instance, a tightly
coupled cluster comprises sensors S2, S3, S6, and S8, with pairwise correlations as high as 0.98 (e.g.
between S2 and S3), indicating significant response redundancy. A second cluster is formed by S4, S5,
and S7, which also exhibit strong inter-correlations (e.g. 0.90 between S4 and S5). In stark contrast,
sensor S1 shows weak correlation with most other elements, indicating that it provides a unique and
non-redundant information channel. This high degree of collinearity presents a challenge for traditional
linear models but also provides an opportunity for a more sophisticated approach to exploit this struc-
ture for robustness. This complex correlation landscape underscores the necessity of a data-driven model
like our proposed deep learning framework, which is capable of autonomously learning to navigate these

2.3.Dataaugmentationthroughtemporalsegmentation
Deep neural networks require large, diverse datasets to learn generalizable features and avoid overfit-
ting, a condition rarely met by the limited number of measurements typically collected in a laboratory
setting [ 45, 46]. T o address the data scarcity inherent in experimental gas sensing and to enhance the
robustness of our deep learning model, we employed a sliding window data augmentation technique. W e
systematically expand the dataset by generating multiple, temporally shifted samples from each original
measurement.
Each original measurement constitutes a time-series capturing the sensor array’s dynamic response to
a gas concentration. The core response, from initial rise to final decay, typically spans approximately 105
data points. So, we defined a slightly larger analysis window of 115 data points to capture the full signal
shape while keeping the data collection time as short as possible, which was systematically translated
across a broader temporal range with a fixed step size of 1 data point encompassing both the primary
response and the adjacent baseline periods. This configuration results in an overlap rate of 99.13% (114
out of 115 points) between consecutive windows. This process segments each original measurement into
overlapping time-series samples, effectively increasing the training dataset size by an order of magnitude.
After augmentation, each gas concentration level contributes 11 samples, resulting in 66 samples per gas
across all six concentrations. Figure 6 shows an example of the sliding window process.
This data augmentation technique serves a dual purpose. First, it introduces temporal translation
invariance. By presenting the model with the gas signature at various positions within the window, it
forces the model to learn the intrinsic shape and features of the response rather than its absolute tem-
poral location. This is a critical feature for real-world deployment, where response timings may vary
due to minor fluctuations in flow rate or environmental conditions. Second, and critically, this method
allows for the systematic generation of samples corresponding to a ‘clean air’ or null class. By extracting
windows exclusively from the baseline periods where no target analyte is present, we train the model
to explicitly distinguish between the presence and absence of a target gas. This represents a signific-
ant methodological improvement over the baseline study [ 33], which did not include an air class, and
is essential for preventing false positives in a practical e-nose system. The resulting augmented data-
set provides the scale and diversity required to train a deep neural network that is both accurate and
generalizable.

2.4.Cross-validation
T o rigorously assess model generalization and ensure the reliability of our performance metrics, we
employed a 5-fold cross-validation scheme. Given the finite size of the experimental dataset, a robust
cross-validation strategy is essential to obtain an unbiased estimate of the model’s performance on
unseen data. A simple random split would be inadequate, as it would not guarantee that the model is
tested on its ability to predict concentrations it has not been trained on. Therefore, we designed a spe-
cialized stratified, leave-one-concentration-out partitioning strategy. The augmented dataset was divided
into five folds. In each iteration, one fold served as the held-out validation/test set, while the other four
were used for training. The construction of these folds was highly structured to prevent informational
leakage and to create a challenging and realistic evaluation scenario: 1) for each of the eight target gases,
a single, unique concentration level was held out for testing in each split. For example, in split 1, the test
set could include concentration level 1 of CO, concentration level 2 of H 2, and concentration level 3 of
NH3, while in split 2, a different combination of concentration levels from each gas was held out, and so
on, for the first five concentration levels, as shown in figure 7; 2) this rotating hold-out strategy ensures
that the model is always evaluated on its ability to interpolate—that is, to predict a concentration value
it has not explicitly seen during training. This is a far more stringent and practical test of generalization
than evaluating on a random subset of known concentrations.

3.Aunifiedframeworkformultitaskgasclassificationandregression
T o address the limitations of classical, disjointed modeling, we developed a unified, end-to-end MT-DL
framework. The core of this framework is a single, cohesive neural network designed to learn directly
from raw sensor time-series data and simultaneously perform two distinct but related tasks: identifying
the gas analyte (classification) and estimating its concentration (regression). The architecture is specific-
ally engineered to enable synergistic learning between these tasks, leading to improved performance and
efficiency, notably by reducing prediction costs and latency through the consolidation of both tasks into
a single forward pass.

3.1.Theproposeddual-branchattentionarchitecture
Our proposed architecture is a dual-branch model with attention-based conditioning, as depicted
in figure 9 and table 2. The model takes the normalized sensor time-series as input and processes it
through two parallel, specialized feature extractors. The top branch, the Classification Head, is respons-
ible for identifying the analyte from a set of N classes (eight gases plus ambient air). It is implemented
as a 1D CNN backbone that processes the input time-series tensor (115 time points × 8 sensor chan-
nels) to extract a latent feature representation. This representation is passed through fully connected
(dense) layers to a final softmax activation layer, which produces a probability distribution over the
N classes. The bottom branch, the Regression Head, is responsible for estimating the concentration of
the gas. It features its own independent 1D CNN backbone, allowing it to learn a feature representa-
tion specifically optimized for the nonlinearities of concentration prediction. The selection of 1D CNN
backbones instead of architectures like LSTM, TCN, or hybrid CNN-LSTM is driven by their superior
ability to autonomously learn hierarchical features from raw thermal fingerprints while preserving full
temporal integrity. This choice provides an optimal balance between high-fidelity pattern recognition
and the stringent resource constraints of Edge AI systems. Unlike models that require heavy computa-
tional overhead to manage complex temporal states—particularly as standard STM32 MCUs currently
lack optimized support for efficient LSTM execution—our unified CNN-based design captures discrim-
inative patterns in the shape and evolution of sensor responses while remaining lightweight. This archi-
tectural efficiency ensures robust, real-time inference on resource-constrained MCUs without the need
for lossy pre-processing, as validated by our successful deployment across 10 different STM32 MCUs.

A ubiquitous challenge in chemical sensing is that analyte concentrations often span several orders of

In this formulation, the absolute error for each sample is inversely scaled by its true concentration
(ytrue), modulated by a tunable exponent, k. By setting k > 0, the loss function assigns a disproportion-

high fidelity where it matters most. The term ε is a small constant added for numerical stability to pre-
vent division by zero.
This design is a flexible generalization of the MAPE concept. When k = 1 and, it becomes equival-

can systematically optimize the loss function’s sensitivity to find the ideal balance for our specific data-
set. The performance of the regression task is critically dependent on the hyperparameters k and ε in
our custom loss function. T o determine their optimal values, we performed an exhaustive grid search.
For each combination of candidate values for k and ε, the full MT-DL model was trained across all
five cross-validation folds, and the average MAPE was computed on the validation sets. The combina-
tion that yielded the lowest average MAPE was selected for the final model evaluation. This data-driven
approach ensures that the chosen hyperparameters are optimized for generalization performance.
3.3.Two-stagetrainingprotocol
Within each fold of our 5-fold cross-validation scheme, we employed a two-stage training process to
optimize the MT-DL model using a batch size of 32 and a maximum of 250 epochs. This two-stage pro-
cess stabilizes training and allows the regression task to benefit from a high-quality, pre-trained feature
representation of the gas identity: 1) Stage 1—Classification Head pre-training: the Classification Head
was trained independently on the gas identification task. The model was optimized using the categorical
cross-entropy loss function and the Adam optimizer. W e employed an adaptive learning rate schedule,
which reduces the learning rate upon plateauing of the validation accuracy, and early model checkpoint-
ing (using a patience of 20 epochs) to save the parameters corresponding to the best validation perform-
ance. This stage ensures the model first becomes an expert at gas identification; 2) Stage 2—full MT-DL
model training: after Stage 1, the weights of the trained Classification Head were frozen. The full MT-DL
framework, including the Regression Head and the attention mechanism, was then trained. The model
was optimized solely on the custom-engineered regression loss function (section 3.2). The same adaptive
learning rate and checkpointing strategies were used, this time monitoring the validation regression loss.

4.4.Ablationstudy:impactofmodelarchitecture
T o validate our choice of the dual-branch attention architecture, we conducted an ablation study com-
paring its performance against two logical alternative multitask designs. The first alternative, a shared
feature extractor model (figure 16), yielded a poor MAPE of 23.98%. This design creates a representa-
tional bottleneck, forcing a single CNN backbone to learn a compromised set of features that are not
optimal for either classification or regression, leading to significant performance degradation. The second
alternative, a dual feature extractor with feature concatenation (figure 17), performed better with a
MAPE of 21.08%. By allowing for specialized feature learning in separate backbones, it overcame the
bottleneck issue. However, the static concatenation of features still limits the model’s ability to adapt.
Our proposed dual-branch attention architecture substantially outperformed both alternatives with a
final MAPE of 6.61%. This demonstrates that the dynamic conditioning provided by the attention mech-
anism is the critical component for success. It allows the model to not only learn specialized features but
also to synergistically combine them in a targeted, adaptive manner based on the identified gas. The res-
ults, summarized in table 4, definitively establish the superiority of our proposed approach.

5.Conclusion
This work introduced and validated a unified, end-to-end MT-DL framework, presenting a definitive
solution to the critical limitations of conventional ML in e-nose systems. By moving beyond reduct-
ive feature engineering and disjointed modeling, our approach demonstrated exceptional performance,
achieving 100% classification accuracy and an average regression MAPE of 6.61%. The success of this
framework is built upon three synergistic innovations. First, by processing the complete temporal fin-
gerprints, our model avoids the lossy pre-processing inherent in classical approaches. Second, our novel
attention-based multitask architecture enabled the synergistic learning of classification and regression,
proving to be substantially more effective than alternative designs. Third, the custom-engineered loss
function proved essential for achieving high-fidelity quantification across the entire dynamic range, a
critical challenge in chemical sensing. The culmination of these design choices is a model that is not
only highly accurate but also, as demonstrated by its successful deployment on 10 STM32 MCUs, com-
putationally efficient enough for practical, real-time embedded applications.
